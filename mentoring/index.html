Mentoring philosophy

When I was 14 and leaving Iran for America, my father gave me advice that has stayed with me throughout my career. He told me that if I were ever fortunate enough to mentor young people, I should treat them in such a way that years later, when they saw me across the street, they would want to cross over and say hello.

My goal as a mentor is to help students discover the joy of living with a scientific puzzle—to find a question compelling enough that they keep thinking about it long after leaving the laboratory. I work closely with students as they learn to design experiments, analyze data, read the literature critically, write papers and proposals, and communicate their ideas.

Mentoring also means investing time in the person, not only the project. Weekly meetings, lab discussions, shared meals, conferences, and introductions to other scientists are opportunities to learn what each trainee hopes to become and to help them get there. As students gain experience, my role shifts toward helping them develop independence and find the scientific and professional path that is right for them.
