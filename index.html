Research to understand neural computations of the cerebellum.

Control of movements by the cerebellum

The cerebellum houses 80% of the neurons in our brain. We study the neurons of the cerebellum to understand what they are computing as the brain performs a movement, or makes plans for a future action.

Purkinje cell spiking during a movement

Purkinje cells are the output neurons of the cerebellar cortex. Here you see and hear the activities of a Purkinje cell during control of tongue movements. Notice that there are two kinds of spikes: simple spikes and complex spikes.

Computation via populations of neurons

It is hard to understand what individual neurons are computing. However, when neurons are organized into populations, important patterns emerge. Here, you see many Purkinje cells recorded simultaneously, allowing us to ask how populations of neurons coordinate their activities.

Multi-layer structure of neurons

Mossy fibers provide input to the cerebellum, while Purkinje cells are the output from the cerebellar cortex. The challenge is to understand how the cerebellum transforms the mossy fiber input into nucleus neuron output.

Example of mossy fiber input

Mossy fibers provide input to the cerebellum, while Purkinje cells are the output from the cerebellar cortex. Here is an example of a Neuropixels Ultra recording during saccadic eye movements.

Climbing fibers are teachers of the cerebellum

A key to understanding neural computation has been the climbing fibers, which produce complex spikes in Purkinje cells. These are low-frequency events that completely suppress the Purkinje cell's ability to produce simple spikes.

We are explorers, aiming to understand the function of the cerebellum. In primates, 80% of the neurons in the brain are located in the cerebellum, and diseases of the cerebellum affect every movement and every thought. We study the neural computations that allow the cerebellum to control movements and contribute to the formation of thoughts. Our principal model is the marmoset, and our tools include neurophysiology and mathematics. As we better understand the computations of the cerebellum, we will build neural implants that can reproduce that computation and replace the diseased cerebellum.
