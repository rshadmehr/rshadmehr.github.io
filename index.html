Research to understand neural computations of the cerebellum.

Control of movements by the cerebellum

The cerebellum houses 80% of the neurons in our brain. We study the neurons of the cerebellum to understand what they are computing as the brain performs a movement, or makes plans for a future action.

Purkinje cell spiking during a movement

Purkinje cells are the output neurons of the cerebellar cortex. Here you see and hear the activities of a Purkinje cell during control of tongue movements. Notice that there are two kinds of spikes: simple spikes and complex spikes. Turn on sound to hear the neuronal spikes.

Computation via populations of neurons

It is hard to understand what individual neurons are computing. However, when neurons are organized into populations, important patterns emerge. Here, you see many Purkinje cells recorded simultaneously, allowing one to ask how populations of neurons coordinate their activities. Turn on sound to hear the neuronal spikes.

Example of mossy fiber input

Mossy fibers provide input to the cerebellum, while Purkinje cells are the output from the cerebellar cortex. Here is an example of a Neuropixels Ultra recording of mossy fibers during saccadic eye movements. Turn on sound to hear the neuronal spikes.

Climbing fibers are teachers of the cerebellum

A key to understanding neural computation has been the climbing fibers, which produce complex spikes in Purkinje cells. These are low-frequency events that briefly suppress the Purkinje cell's ability to produce simple spikes. Turn on sound to hear the neuronal spikes.

Example of a nucleus neuron

Here is a Neuropixels recording of a nucleus neuron, the output region of the cerebellum. This neuron is strongly modulated by saccades. Try with sound on.

We are explorers, aiming to understand the function of the cerebellum. In primates, diseases of the cerebellum affect every movement and every thought. We study the neural computations that allow the cerebellum to control movements and contribute to the formation of thoughts. Our principal model is the marmoset, and our tools include neurophysiology and mathematics. Our long-term goal is to understand cerebellar computation well enough that one day it may be possible to reproduce these functions in neural prosthetic devices.
