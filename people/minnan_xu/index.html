Minnan Xu

PhD (2010)

Google Scholar

Research

Minnan completed her BS and MS in Electrical Engineering from MIT and then joined the lab in 2005. Minnan built the electronics and control harware for the two-arm robot in our lab. The instruments that she designed and constructed made it possible for us to measure bimanual control.

She discovered that stimulus value affected vigor of saccadic eye movements. This demonstrated that the way the brain evaluated economic quantities like reward and effort was reflected in vigor of movements. She discovered that a function of the cerebellum was to monitor the variability in motor commands and compensate for it through internal feedback. Minnan also discovered that the fast timescale of adaptation was impaired in cerebellar disease. Finally, Minnan discovered that transcranial magnetic stimulation of the brain could disrupt an ongoing saccade, but that this perturbation was compensated with motor commands that arrived later in the same movement.

She completed a PhD in Biomedical Engineering on May 2010. She subsequently became a postdoc at the Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary, Harvard University. She is now Director of AI/ML at Takeda. ​

Ideas developed by Minnan

  • Providing evidence for internal feedback control of saccades. Minnan helped show that saccadic eye movements are controlled by an internal feedback mechanism that continuously compares the evolving movement with its desired outcome. By perturbing the system with TMS, her work revealed corrective processes that normally remain hidden during the very rapid production of a saccade.
  • Defining the role of the cerebellum in adaptive control of saccades. Minnan studied how people with cerebellar dysfunction adapt their saccadic eye movements. Her work helped establish the contribution of the cerebellum to learning and maintaining the internal mechanisms that ensure accurate control of saccades.
  • Showing that the value of visual information influences movement vigor. Minnan helped demonstrate that saccade velocity depends not only on the physical requirements of the movement, but also on the value of the visual information that the movement will acquire. This work helped link the vigor of an eye movement to the brain's valuation of its expected outcome.
  • Linking the cost of time to the control of movement. Minnan contributed to the idea that the nervous system assigns a cost to the passage of time because delayed rewards lose value. This work helped provide a theoretical framework in which movement vigor reflects the tradeoff between energetic costs and the cost of time.
