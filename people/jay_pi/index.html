Jay Pi

PhD (2024)

Google Scholar

Research

Jay joined the lab in 2018 as an PhD student in the BME program, after receiving a BS in Mechanical Engineering, as well as a BS in Neurobiology, both with the highest honors from the University of Texas, Austin. He had a wide range of undergraduate research experiences, from studying the effects of viral infections on drug metabolism to the manufacturing design of mechanical systems. ​ Jay wrote the entire behavioral software system that we use in training our marmosets. He then recorded activities of neurons in the cerebellar cortex during a task in which the subjects experienced an error, but had the option of either correcting that error, or simply observe it and withhold their correction. He discovered that the climbing fiber input to the cerebellum encoded both the direction of a random sensory event, as well as the direction of a forthcoming voluntary movement. Surprisingly, these two kinds of information were transmiteed to different Purkinje cells. His work suggests that cerebellar neurons that learn from different teachers (climbing fibers) contribute to different aspects of behavior. Jay discovered that in the oculomotor region of the vermis, the olive informed a subset of P-cells about sensory events that were prediction errors, but all P-cells about the forthcoming movement. Using the information content of the olivary input he labeled the P-cells and produced a population code, revealing two groups with simple spikes that were antagonistic to each other, one contributing to movement initiation, the other signaling movement end. Jay graduated in 2024 and subsequently joined a venture capital technology group.

Ideas developed by Jay

  • Demonstrating that climbing fiber signals distinguish sensory events from movement plans. Jay investigated how the inferior olive conveys information to the cerebellum during visually guided movements. His work showed that climbing fiber responses distinguish sensory events from planned movements, providing evidence that complex spikes carry information beyond a simple representation of sensory prediction errors. These findings helped clarify the information available to Purkinje cells for motor control and learning.
  • Revealing the causal influence of complex spikes on movement. Jay contributed to research demonstrating that complex spikes can perturb ongoing movements. By measuring the behavioral consequences of individual complex spikes, this work helped identify the sensorimotor influence of Purkinje cells and revealed a map linking their activity to movement direction. These findings suggested that complex spikes are not merely signals for learning but can also directly influence motor output.
  • Investigating how synchronous Purkinje cell spiking contributes to motor control. Jay contributed to experiments examining the coordination of spike timing among cerebellar Purkinje cells during movement. This work demonstrated that Purkinje cells exhibit synchronous activity during behavior, suggesting that the timing of spikes across populations of neurons may be important for controlling movements, in addition to changes in their average firing rates.
  • Investigating cerebellar contributions to the deceleration of tongue movements. Jay contributed to research examining Purkinje cell activity during tongue movements in behaving marmosets. This work demonstrated that Purkinje cells contribute to the control of tongue deceleration, extending the study of cerebellar motor control to movements involved in licking and feeding.
  • Developing experimental and analytical approaches for cerebellar neurophysiology. Jay contributed to the development of P-sort, an open-source software package for analyzing simple and complex spikes in extracellular Purkinje cell recordings. He also participated in behavioral experiments examining how effort costs influence foraging decisions and movement vigor in marmosets. These contributions helped expand the experimental approaches available for investigating cerebellar function during behavior.
