Jorn Diedrichsen

Postdoctoral Fellow (2003-2006)

Google Scholar

Research

Jörn joined the lab in 2003 after completing his PhD in Psychology from University of California Berkeley. With Jörn, we built an fMRI compatible robot and became the first lab in the world to study reach adaptation using functional magnetic resonance imaging. Jörn brought the tools of statistical learning to the problem of motor adaptation, altering the path of motor learning research to incorporate optimal estimation and control. He subsequently became an Assistant Professor at University of Bangor, UK, then Associate Professor at University College London. He is currently Research Chair for Motor Control and Computational Neuroscience, University of Western Ontario.

Ideas developed by Jorn

  • Establishing sensory prediction error as a driver of motor adaptation. Jörn's work helped demonstrate that adaptation of reaching is driven by sensory prediction errors—the difference between the predicted and observed consequences of an action—and that this learning critically depends on the cerebellum.
  • Clarifying the role of the cerebellum in coordination. His experiments helped distinguish deficits in the coordination of multijoint movements from deficits in movement timing, supporting the idea that a central function of the cerebellum is to predict and compensate for the dynamics that link different components of a movement.
  • Identifying neural representations of movement errors. Using functional imaging, Jörn helped distinguish brain activity associated with different kinds of reaching errors, providing insight into how the nervous system represents errors that can subsequently guide changes in motor commands.
  • Developing tools for studying the human cerebellum with neuroimaging. Jörn developed an unbiased anatomical template of the human cerebellum and methods for detecting and correcting artifacts in fMRI data. These methodological contributions provided important tools for more accurate measurement and localization of human cerebellar activity.
