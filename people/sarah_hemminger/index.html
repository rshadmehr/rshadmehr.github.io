Sarah Hemminger

PhD (2010)

Google Scholar

Research

Sarah joined the laboratory as an undergraduate student in 2001 while she was completing her BS in Biomedical Engineering at Johns Hopkins University. As an undergraduate she performed the first motor adaptation experiment on a split-brain patient, demonstrating that inter-manual transfer occurred despite disconnection of the two hemispheres.

She joined the BME PhD program in 2005 and discovered a latent form of motor adaptation in patients with severe cerebellar damage. This was a breakthrough because until then, we thought that cerebellar damage made it impossible for the brain to learn motor control. She also discovered that sudden performance errors in a well-learned task did not produce unlearning, but installed a new, fragile memory that with passage of time became strengthened. Sarah was awarded a pre-doctoral fellowship (NRSA) from the National Institutes of Health in 2008. She was awareded the Siebel Prize in Bioengineering in 2009. She completed her PhD in 01/2010.

In addition to her numerous scientific discoveries, as a graduate student Sarah made tremendous contributions to the Baltimore community. She organized the graduate and medical students to mentor the worst-performing high school students in the nearby schools. Her work transformed the lives of many young people, for which she was awarded fellowships from Echoing Green Foundation, Open Society Institute, and the Albert Schweitzer Fellows Program, which support social entrepreneurs with innovative ideas. She is the cofounder and CEO of Thread Inc. ​

Ideas developed by Sarah

  • Revealing multiple timescales in the formation and consolidation of motor memories. Sarah studied how newly acquired motor memories change with the passage of time. Her work helped show that motor memories contain processes with different timescales and that their stability and expression depend on when they are tested after learning.
  • Defining how the cerebellum uses movement errors to drive motor learning. Sarah helped show that the contribution of the cerebellum to adaptation depends on the size and nature of the errors experienced during training. Her work helped establish sensory prediction error as an important teaching signal for cerebellum-dependent learning.
  • Distinguishing the roles of the cerebellum and motor cortex in motor learning. Sarah contributed to studies showing that the cerebellum and primary motor cortex make different contributions to adaptation and motor memory. This work helped separate mechanisms involved in learning from error from those involved in the retention and expression of the resulting motor memory.
  • Showing how motor memories generalize and are protected from interference. Sarah investigated how learning acquired with one arm can generalize to the other arm and how previously acquired motor memories are protected when new learning takes place. Her work helped characterize how the nervous system organizes, retains, and expresses multiple motor memories.
