Robert Geary

MS (2018)

Research

Rob joined the lab in 2017 as a BME MS student, after studying Electrical Engineering at MIT and Boston University. Following graduation from college, he worked in the fields of missile defense, medical research, and translational neuroscience. He is an Eagle Scout, and a jazz musician. Rob's research in our lab focused on the effects of reward and effort on the utility of eye movements. He demonstrated that as the rate of reward increased, movement vigor also increased, while reaction-time decreased. This result suggests that during gazing, information is harvested by the brain, raising as a function that is concave downward. Rob completed his research in summer of 2018, and subsequently joined the PhD program at Harvard University.

Ideas developed by Rob

  • Linking decision-making and movement vigor through a common principle of optimal control. Robert contributed to research showing that decisions about how long to remain at a rewarding location and how quickly to move to the next location may be governed by a shared computational principle. By extending optimal foraging theory to include the energetic costs of movement, this work provided a unified framework for understanding both decisions and motor control.
  • Demonstrating that movement vigor depends on the history and expectation of reward. Robert helped design and conduct experiments in which people viewed rewarding images and made eye movements between them. The experiments showed that the history of reward and expectations of future reward influenced both gaze duration and saccade speed. These findings suggested that the nervous system adjusts movement vigor according to the overall richness of the environment, rather than simply the immediate demands of the movement.
  • Investigating how effort shapes decisions and movement speed. Robert contributed to experiments examining how the effort required to acquire reward influences behavior. The results showed that effort affected both the time spent harvesting information and the speed of subsequent eye movements. Although many findings agreed with optimal foraging theory, the observation that people increased movement vigor after experiencing high effort revealed an important limitation of the model.
