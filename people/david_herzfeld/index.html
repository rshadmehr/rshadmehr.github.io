David Herzfeld

PhD (2016)

Google Scholar

Research

David joined the lab in 2011 as a PhD student in the BME program, after completing a BS and MS from Marquette University. David was an Allen Edmonds Scholar, Anthony and Rose Bagoszzi Medical Research Fellow, and a Richard Jobling Fellow during his years at Marquette University. His research in our laboratory focused on the function of the cerebellum. He discovered the theory of memory of errors, which suggests that during learning, the brain not only learns from a perdiction error, but also stores a memory of the error event itself. He solved two fundamental problems: how Purkinje cells of the cerebellum anatomically organize into computational units that predict motion of the body, and how these cells learn from their prediction error and alter behavior on subsequent movements.

During his tenure as a graduate student, he received an NRSA award from the NIH in 2014. In 2015 he was awarded the Mette Strand Award for outstanding research by a graduate student at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine. In 2016 he was awarded the Siebel Prize in Bioengineering. He won the 2016 David Yue Award for outstanding research by a BME PhD student. In 2017, he was awarded the The Donald B. Lindsley Prize in Behavioral Neuroscience by the Society for Neuroscience. This award recognizes a young neuroscientist's outstanding PhD thesis.

In addition to his scientific accomplishments, David made tremendous contributions to our laboratory, as well as our community of scientist at Hopkins. He completely rewrote the low-level code that we employ to control all of our instruments, including our robots and eye-tracking systems. He wrote the software for behavioral and neurophysiological data acquisition in our non-human primate studies. In addition, he wrote the web-based database management system that the Johns Hopkins BME PhD Program uses to monitor progress of the graduate students. He completed his thesis in 2016, and next trained as a postdoc with Steve Lisberger at Duke University. David was subsequently appointed Asst. Prof. of Neuroscience at the University of Wisconsin.

Ideas developed by David

  • Discovering that the brain maintains a memory of errors. David helped show that the amount we learn from an error depends on the history of errors that preceded it. When errors are consistent, the nervous system becomes more sensitive to them; when errors are inconsistent, sensitivity decreases. This introduced the idea that error sensitivity itself is learned and provides an important mechanism for adapting the rate of motor learning.
  • Revealing how Purkinje cells transform errors into learning. David helped identify how climbing-fiber-driven complex spikes encode movement errors and how those errors subsequently alter Purkinje-cell simple-spike activity. This work provided a neural link between an error experienced on one movement and the corrective change in motor output on the next movement.
  • Showing how populations of Purkinje cells encode movement. David's work demonstrated that individual Purkinje cells can have complex patterns of activity, yet their population activity can be organized according to their influence on behavior. This provided evidence that coordinated activity across Purkinje cells generates commands that control the kinematics of movement.
  • Clarifying the distinct roles of the cerebellum and motor cortex in motor memory. David contributed to work showing that the cerebellum and motor cortex make different contributions to motor learning, helping distinguish neural mechanisms involved in the acquisition of a motor memory from those involved in its retention.
