Pavan Vaswani

MD/PhD (2014)

Google Scholar

Research

Pavan joined the lab in 2011 as an MD/PhD student, after completing a BS in Computer Science, Neurobiology and Biochemistry from University of Washington. Pavan is a Goldwater, Gates, and Washington Scholar. Pavan's research demonstrated that trial-to-trial forgetting, a property of motor memory, is the reason why adaptation is often incomplete. Using optimal control theory, he investigated the question of hypometria and segmentation of movements in both healthy people and people with certain movement disorders. He discovered that these behaviors were a compensation that the brain had made in response to noise in the oculomotor system. Working with Jennie Choi, Pavan investigated the question of why some people exhibit high movement vigor (moving their eyes with high velocity), while others exhibit low movement vigor. He was able to link movement vigor to the reward system of the brain, suggesting that high vigor may be associated with steep temporal discounting of reward. This suggested that trait-like features of individuality, such as impulsivity, are related to trait-like features of movement, namely vigor. Pavan completed his dissertation in 2014. He subsequently returned to the medical school, completed the MD curriculum in 2016, and enrolled in the neurology residency program at Harvard.​ He subsequently was appointed Asst. Professor of Neurology at the Univ. of Pennsylvania School of Medicine.

Ideas developed by Pavan

  • Discovering that the brain maintains a memory of past errors. Pavan helped show that learning from an error depends on the history of errors experienced during previous movements. Rather than having a fixed sensitivity to error, the motor system increases or decreases how much it learns from particular errors based on their consistency in the recent past.
  • Characterizing how motor memories decay. Pavan showed that motor memories can decay even when movements are performed without error. His work helped distinguish processes that maintain a learned motor command from those that update behavior in response to error.
  • Explaining why residual errors persist during motor adaptation. Pavan investigated why people often stop correcting their movements before an error has been completely eliminated. His work showed that persistent errors can reflect competing processes, including a tendency to revert toward baseline behavior and exploratory changes in action when errors remain.
  • Linking movement vigor to reward and the cost of time. Pavan contributed to the idea that movement speed reflects the value assigned to time. His work helped show that the vigor of an action is related to the temporal cost of obtaining its outcome, linking principles of decision-making and reward to the control of movement.
