Opher Donchin

Whittaker Distinguished Postdoctoral Fellow (2000)

Google Scholar

Research

Opher joined the lab in 2000, after completing a PhD in Computational Neuroscience from Hebrew University, and a BS in Mathematics from MIT. He won a Whitaker Distinguished Postdoctoral Fellowship from the Biomedical Engineering program at Johns Hopkins. Opher's research led to extension of the state-space approach to quantify generalization patterns in motor learning. He demonstrated that adaptation was not a process of disturbance rejection, but a process of optimization. He generously gave his time to help the research of many students, including Maurice Smith, Sarah Hemminger and EunJung Hwang. He subsequently became Assistant Professor of BME at Ben Gurion University, Israel, and then Professor and Chair of the BME Dept. at Ben Gurion University.

Ideas developed by Opher

  • Developing a computational framework for motor learning and generalization. Opher helped establish the idea that motor adaptation can be understood as function approximation with basis functions, and developed methods for using trial-by-trial behavior to infer the generalization properties of the underlying representations.
  • Understanding the formation, retention, and contextual organization of motor memories. His experiments examined how motor memories are acquired and recalled, and how contextual information, including movement direction and serial order, allows the nervous system to organize and retrieve different internal models.
  • Characterizing what is represented in an internal model. His work helped show that learned representations of arm dynamics depend jointly on limb position and velocity, consistent with a gain-field-like representation. He also studied how learned dynamics generalize between the two arms and whether learning alters the desired movement trajectory.
  • Connecting adaptation to prediction and optimization. His work contributed to the view that the motor system uses an internal estimate of the state of the body to guide ongoing behavior. Later work helped distinguish adaptation of an internal model from reoptimization of behavior in response to learning.
