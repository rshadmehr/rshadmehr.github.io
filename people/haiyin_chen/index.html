Haiyin Chen-Harris

PhD (2006)

Google Scholar

Research

Haiyin graduated with a BS from Princeton University and then joined the lab as a PhD student in the BME Program. She was the first graduate student in our lab to perform research using human psychophysics combined with human single cell neurophysiology (experiments that were conducted in the Hopkins operating rooms). Before Haiyin, we focused entirely on study of reaching movement. However, Haiyin added the study of saccadic eye movements and through examination of patients with cerebellar damage, discovered that during a single saccade, the cerebellum steers the eyes to its intended destination through internal monitoring of the motor commands. She demonstrated that a similar organization existed during reaching movements, and that its anatomy was likely through cerebello-thalamic pathways to the cerebral cortex. She completed her PhD in 2006, and subsequently became a research scientist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, then Staff Scientist at Genentech. She is currently Staff Scientist at Microsoft Research.

Ideas developed by Haiyin

  • Demonstrating that the brain uses adaptive internal feedback to control saccades. Haiyin showed that the brain can correct eye movements while they are in progress, despite the absence of useful visual feedback. She discovered that saccade adaptation involves two processes: a fast-learning, rapidly forgetting process that modifies commands late in the movement, and a slower-learning, better-retained process that modifies the initial motor commands. Subsequent experiments in patients with cerebellar degeneration demonstrated that the cerebellum is particularly important for the fast adaptive process.
  • Establishing the importance of the cerebellar–thalamic pathway in motor adaptation. Haiyin studied patients receiving deep brain stimulation of the cerebellar thalamus to treat essential tremor. She found that although stimulation reduced tremor, it also impaired the ability to adapt reaching movements to novel forces. Patients with thalamic lesions exhibited similar deficits. These findings provided evidence that the cerebellar–thalamic–cortical pathway is essential for learning internal models of movement dynamics.
